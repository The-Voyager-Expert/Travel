Day 1
From Hotel: 🚶 18 min · 🚕 9 min → Charles Darwin Research Station
1.Charles Darwin Research Station
The oldest scientific hub in the Galápagos, where captive-bred giant tortoises from seven subspecies are raised in outdoor breeding pens before repatriation to their home islands — best known as the former home of Lonesome George and now Diego, who fathered over 800 offspring during his decades in the breeding program.

2.Las Ninfas Lagoon
A brackish tidal lagoon edged by red mangroves just beyond the research station — the narrow boardwalk path above the water reveals whitetip reef sharks resting on the sandy bottom, sea turtles gliding beneath, and marine iguanas draped over exposed roots in the shallows.

Day 2
From Hotel: 🚶 8 min · 🚕 5 min → Las Grietas
1.Las Grietas
A narrow seawater-filled lava cleft where the crystal-clear channel runs between volcanic walls rising 10 m on each side — eagle rays, yellow-tailed surgeonfish, and the occasional sea lion pass through, making this the most accessible open-water snorkelling in Santa Cruz without booking a guided excursion.

2.Tortuga Bay
A 1.5 km crescent of powdery white sand framed by prickly-pear cactus along a paved lava trail — Playa Brava faces the open Pacific with surf that draws nesting sea turtles to the dark-sand western point, while the calmer Playa Mansa around the headland is sheltered enough for swimming alongside marine iguanas in astonishing numbers.

3.North Seymour Island Day Excursion
A flat, uplifted island 25 km north of Santa Cruz where blue-footed boobies perform sky-pointing courtship dances at eye level on the trail, magnificent frigatebirds inflate scarlet throat pouches in shoreline shrubs, land iguanas bask on basalt at the end of the circuit, and sea lions occupy every rock along the beach — the single most concentrated wildlife-viewing spot in the Galápagos.

Day 3
From Hotel: 🚕 20 min → Los Gemelos
1.Los Gemelos
Two collapsed volcanic calderas — each roughly 30 m deep — sunken into highland Scalesia forest on either side of the Vía Baltra highway, where woodpecker finches probe dead bark with cactus-spine tools, Galápagos short-eared owls hunt at dusk, and the dense canopy of moss-draped tree ferns provides the islands' only true cloud-forest atmosphere.

2.El Chato Tortoise Reserve
A private working ranch in the Santa Cruz highlands where wild Galápagos giant tortoises — some carrying more than a century on their carapace — graze freely through muddy wallows and native grass meadows at 300 m elevation, offering the most unhurried and crowd-free tortoise encounter on the island.

3.El Chato Lava Tunnels
A network of subterranean tubes formed when the outer crust of a lava flow solidified while molten rock drained away beneath — dark enough to need a torch, narrow enough to touch both basalt walls simultaneously, creating a sharp and memorable contrast to the open highland pasture above.

4.Garrapatero Beach
A protected white-sand cove on Santa Cruz's northeastern shore reached by a dirt road through farmland — a small brackish lagoon behind the sand bar attracts flamingos and white-cheeked pintails in the late afternoon, the calm bay offers safer swimming than exposed Tortuga Bay, and the rocky eastern point has accessible reef snorkelling.

🍮 Local Tastes
Ceviche de Galápagos
Fresh corvina or tuna marinated in citrus juice with red onion, tomato, and ají amarillo — lighter and less creamy than the Peruvian version, served with plantain chips or tostado and a side of popcorn in most local spots.
📍 La Garrapata · Puerto Ayora
Langosta a la Plancha
Whole Galápagos spiny lobster grilled over charcoal and finished with garlic butter; served from June to November when the regulated fishery opens — the sweetest catch in the archipelago, cooked simply to let the flavor stand.
📍 Isla Grill at Angermeyer Waterfront Inn · Puerto Ayora
Encebollado
Ecuador's national breakfast soup — albacore tuna simmered with yuca, tomato, and cumin in a clear broth, topped with pickled red onion and served with plantain chips; available from 7am at local cafés before excursions.
📍 El Descanso del Guía · Puerto Ayora
Bolon de Verde
Savory balls of mashed green plantain stuffed with cheese or slow-cooked pork, eaten for breakfast or as a snack; the Galápagos version tends to be smaller and crisper than the mainland style, often served with a fried egg on top.
📍 TJ Restaurant & Bar · Puerto Ayora
Patacones con Atún
Double-fried green plantain rounds used as a base for fresh tuna tartare or spiced tuna salad — a fusion starter unique to the island's more creative kitchens and a good entry point for visitors not ready to order ceviche.
📍 Lo & Lo · Puerto Ayora

🐢 The Galápagos Pace

The Galápagos does something unusual to a travel itinerary: it slows it down. Not because there is less to do, but because wildlife operates on its own schedule and the islands enforce a rule that most heavily-visited places quietly abandon — the animal has the right of way. A blue-footed booby landing on the trail ahead of you is not an obstacle; it is the event. A sea lion stretched across the path to the dock is not blocking your route; it is your route.

This is most visible at the Charles Darwin Research Station, where giant tortoises that are likely older than anyone visiting them move through the enclosure at a pace that rewards sitting still. Diego, the Española tortoise whose breeding success helped rescue his subspecies, does not perform for visitors. He simply continues being an extraordinarily old tortoise. Spending an hour there instead of twenty minutes changes what you take away from it.

The water taxi crossings are an underrated part of this guide. The five-minute ride across Pelican Bay to Las Grietas is one of the most concentrated wildlife encounters in the archipelago — marine iguanas on the far shore, pelicans in low formation overhead, the occasional sea lion surfacing beside the boat. It is not a detour; it is part of the stop.

North Seymour is the day that reveals what the Galápagos genuinely is: not a place where you go looking for wildlife, but a place where wildlife is simply present, going about its work while you happen to be there. The frigatebird inflating its throat pouch a metre from the trail is not displaying for you. The boobies doing their sky-pointing dance at the base of a cactus are not performing. It is the absence of fear that makes the experience different from every other wildlife encounter on earth, and that absence cannot be recreated or scheduled.
