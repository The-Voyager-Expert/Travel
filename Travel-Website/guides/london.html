From Hotel: 40 min · 12 min → Shakespeare's Globe
1. Shakespeare's Globe
A meticulous reconstruction of the 1599 Globe Theatre, built 230 metres from the original site and opened in 1997. The oak-framed, thatched structure — the first thatched roof permitted in London since the Great Fire of 1666 — holds 860 seated and 700 standing in the yard. Guided tours run daily.

Opened in 2000 in the converted Bankside Power Station, the Tate Modern holds the UK's national collection of international modern and contemporary art from 1900 to the present. The Turbine Hall — 35 metres high — hosts commissions on a scale no other gallery can match. The most visited art museum in the UK.

Christopher Wren's baroque masterpiece completed after the Great Fire destroyed its medieval predecessor. The dome — third largest in the world — defines London's skyline. The Whispering Gallery · Stone Gallery · and Golden Gallery climb 528 steps to panoramic views. Wellington and Nelson are buried in the crypt.

4. Monument to the Great Fire
A Doric column 202 feet tall — exactly the distance from its base to the bakery in Pudding Lane where the Great Fire of 1666 started. Designed by Wren and Hooke · it is the world's tallest isolated stone column. Climbing the 311 steps rewards with close-up views of the City's financial towers.

A Royal Navy Town-class light cruiser commissioned in 1939, moored permanently on the Thames as an Imperial War Museum ship since 1971. In June 1944 HMS Belfast fired nearly 2,000 rounds from her six-inch guns supporting the Allied landings at Normandy. Nine decks are open to visitors.

4. Houses of Parliament
Charles Barry and Augustus Pugin's Gothic Revival palace · rebuilt after the 1834 fire that destroyed the medieval original. Westminster Hall survived — begun in 1097 · its hammerbeam roof is the largest medieval timber roof in northern Europe. Guided tours cross the Central Lobby into the green Commons chamber and the red-and-gold Lords chamber. The Elizabeth Tower holds Big Ben.

1. Churchill War Rooms
The underground bunker from which Churchill and the War Cabinet ran the Second World War · sealed in August 1945 and left untouched for decades. Pin-studded wall charts still record the convoy positions of the final day; Churchill's bedroom holds the microphone he broadcast from. The adjoining Churchill Museum traces the whole life around the wartime years.

2. Westminster Abbey
The coronation church of England since 1066 and the burial place of seventeen monarchs. Henry VII's Lady Chapel carries a fan vault that Washington Irving called a gossamer of stone. Poets' Corner holds Chaucer · Dickens · and Hardy; Newton and Darwin lie in the nave. Every English coronation for nearly a thousand years has taken place on the same Cosmati pavement before the high altar.

5. St James's Park
The oldest of the Royal Parks · drained from marshland by Henry VIII in 1532 and redesigned by John Nash in 1827. The Blue Bridge over the lake gives the two best sightlines in London: Buckingham Palace to the west · the Horse Guards rooftops and the London Eye to the east. Pelicans have lived here since a Russian ambassador gifted a pair in 1664; they are fed at the lakeside around 2:30pm.

A Norman fortress begun by William the Conqueror in 1078 and expanded across nine centuries into a royal palace · armoury · treasury · and prison. The Crown Jewels — 23,578 gemstones including the Cullinan I and the Imperial State Crown — are held in the Jewel House under armed guard. Yeoman Warders have guarded the walls since 1485 and still live inside them.

4. Royal Observatory
Built by Charles II in 1675 to solve the problem of longitude at sea · the Royal Observatory sits atop Greenwich Hill with commanding views of the Thames. The Prime Meridian — longitude 0° — runs through the courtyard. The red Time Ball drops daily at 1pm · a signal sailors used to set their chronometers.

Local Tastes
Full English Breakfast
Bacon · eggs · sausage · black pudding · beans · grilled tomato · toast — the definitive English fry-up eaten at a Formica counter with strong tea. London's oldest greasy-spoon cafés do it best; avoid hotel versions.
Regency Café · E. Pellicci
Fish & Chips
Battered cod or haddock with thick-cut chips · malt vinegar · salt · and mushy peas — Britain's national dish. The batter should be crisp · the fish fresh · the chips fat. Avoid tourist-strip chippies; seek out third-generation operators.
Poppies Fish & Chips · Golden Hind · Rock & Sole Plaice
Afternoon Tea
Finger sandwiches · scones with clotted cream and jam · petit fours · and a pot of tea — served between 3:00pm and 5:00pm in grand hotel dining rooms. The ritual has not changed in 180 years. Book weeks ahead at the classic addresses.
Fortnum & Mason · The Ritz · Claridge's
Pie & Mash
Working-class East End staple since the 1860s — minced beef pie with mashed potato and liquor: a parsley-and-eel-broth sauce. Still served at Victorian-tiled pie-and-mash shops that have not changed since the Victorian era.
M. Manze · G. Kelly's

Worth Knowing

A King's Last Steps, A Monument That Doubled as a Telescope

Banqueting House looks like a grand party venue, and for most of its history it was one — but it's also the last building Charles I ever walked through. On the morning of his execution in January 1649, he was led across the same hall, under Rubens ceiling paintings commissioned by his own father, then out through a first-floor window onto a scaffold built against the building's front. He'd asked for an extra-thick shirt that morning, worried the winter cold might make him shiver and look, to the crowd below, like fear.

The Monument to the Great Fire isn't just a memorial — it was designed to double as scientific equipment. Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke built a hidden laboratory into the base, with a hollow central shaft running the full height of the column up to the viewing platform, so the whole structure could function as a giant zenith telescope pointed straight up at the sky. Hooke was hoping to find observational proof that the Earth orbits the sun. It never worked — traffic rumbling past on the street below shook the column just enough to blur every reading he tried to take.
