Read About Granada

The last Moorish kingdom in Spain fell here in 1492 — and left behind the single most visited monument in the country.

The Last Emirate

Granada was the capital of the Emirate of Granada, the final independent Muslim kingdom on the Iberian Peninsula, ruled by the Nasrid dynasty for two and a half centuries after the rest of Al-Andalus had fallen to Christian reconquest. While Córdoba and Seville were retaken in the 13th century, Granada survived — a tributary state paying homage to Castile, buying time with diplomacy while its rulers built the Alhambra into the most refined expression of Islamic art and architecture in Western Europe.

That survival ended on January 2, 1492, when Boabdil, the last Nasrid sultan, surrendered the city to Ferdinand and Isabella, the Catholic Monarchs. Legend places Boabdil's final look back at his lost kingdom at a mountain pass now called El Suspiro del Moro — the Moor's Sigh. His mother is said to have told him: "You do well to weep like a woman for what you could not defend like a man."

"Dale limosna, mujer, que no hay en la vida nada como la pena de ser ciego en Granada" — give him alms, woman, for there is nothing in life like the sorrow of being blind in Granada. (Francisco de Icaza, on a blind beggar outside the Alhambra)

A City Built in Two Layers

Granada reads as two cities stacked on top of each other. The Moorish city survives almost intact in the Albaicín, the hillside quarter of whitewashed houses and hidden carmen gardens facing the Alhambra across the Darro valley — one of the best-preserved historic Islamic quarters anywhere in Europe, spared largely because it was too poor and too steep to be worth demolishing during later centuries of urban renewal. The Christian city was built directly on top of what the Moors left behind: the Cathedral rises on the site of Granada's Great Mosque, and the Capilla Real holds the tombs of the two monarchs who ended Muslim rule in Spain, buried a few hundred meters from the palace they conquered.

Sacromonte, the third district worth understanding, grew as a settlement of cave-dwellings carved into the hillside beyond Albaicín, home for centuries to Granada's Roma community and the birthplace of a distinct style of flamenco performed inside the caves themselves — zambra gitana, still danced there today.

What the Alhambra Actually Is

The word Alhambra comes from the Arabic al-qal'a al-hamra — "the red fortress," a reference to the reddish clay used in its outer walls. It is not one building but a walled citadel containing a military fortress (the Alcazaba), a set of interconnected royal residences (the Nasrid Palaces), a summer palace and garden retreat (the Generalife), and — jarringly — a Renaissance palace inserted by the Christian monarchs a generation after the conquest (the Palace of Charles V). Visiting all of it in a single pass is why the Alhambra ticket carries a printed, non-negotiable timed entry slot: unmanaged crowds would overwhelm the fragile stucco and tilework of the Nasrid Palaces within a decade.

UNESCO inscribed the Alhambra, Generalife, and Albaicín together as a single World Heritage Site in 1984 — a rare case of a monument and the neighborhood facing it being recognized as one continuous cultural landscape.
