Read About Busan

South Korea's second city was nearly the last city standing — and everything about it, from the hillside refugee settlements to the deepwater port, still carries the weight of that fact.

THE WAR CITY

In September 1950, the Korean War had compressed the entire Republic of Korea into a perimeter around a single southern port. The Pusan Perimeter — a 140-kilometer defensive line — was the last ground the UN-backed forces held. Behind that line was Busan. The city took in approximately one million refugees within a matter of months, roughly tripling its pre-war population. People built wherever they could, on hillsides that had never been intended for habitation, using scrapped wood, salvaged corrugated metal, and whatever came to hand. The Gamcheon settlement — today celebrated as the Gamcheon Culture Village — was one of these. Its terraced houses, stacked up a south-facing slope above Saha-gu, were built by followers of the Taegukdo religious sect who had fled north Korea ahead of the advancing forces. What looks to contemporary visitors like a charming hillside neighborhood is, in its original structure, a wartime refugee camp that happened to survive.

The city that absorbed a nation's displaced in a single season still shows you, if you look carefully, exactly where they put down.

The war ended in an armistice in 1953, but it never formally ended — the Demilitarized Zone is still a ceasefire line, not a peace border. The UN Memorial Cemetery in Nam-gu, the only operational UN military cemetery in the world, maintains 2,300 graves from 11 countries. It is maintained by the United Nations and open to visitors daily. The surrounding city has grown past it — apartment towers press in on every side — but inside the perimeter the grounds remain formally kept, the graves precisely aligned. The Commonwealth section, the Turkish section, the American graves: each reflects the particular form of military honor their countries brought with them in 1950. It is not a tourist attraction in any usual sense. It is a working memorial to a war that has not officially ended.

THE PORT

Busan's natural harbor — a deep, sheltered bay at the southeastern tip of the Korean Peninsula, protected by Yeongdo Island — was why the city existed before any of the rest of it. The Japanese colonial administration developed the port aggressively from the early twentieth century, dredging the harbor and building the rail connection to Seoul that made Busan the entry point for goods moving through the peninsula. After the Korean War, South Korea's government made the deliberate choice to develop Busan as an export manufacturing base. The resulting industrial scale is visible from most of the coastal vantage points in the guide — Taejongdae's cliffs, Dongbaekseom's promenade, the Busan Tower observation deck. The container ships moving through the harbor are not scenery. Busan's port handles roughly 22 million twenty-foot-equivalent container units per year, making it consistently the fifth or sixth busiest port in the world. The South Korean economy — one of the world's largest export economies — runs in part through this harbor.

The port's wartime role was the same as its economic role: the last exit, the last entry point. The final evacuation of UN forces from the north was through Hungnam harbor on the east coast, but the massive refugee exodus of civilians — 100,000 people crossing by sea in the Hungnam Evacuation of December 1950 — came to Busan. The ships that brought the refugees in are the same basic infrastructure that now moves Samsung televisions and Hyundai cars out.

THE MOUNTAIN

Geumjeongsan — the mountain that runs along the northern edge of the city — is a fact of Busan's daily life that most visitors to the southern coastal neighborhoods never encounter. The city is built on flat land between mountains and sea, and Geumjeongsan's ridge forms the northern horizon from much of central Busan. The fortress built on that ridge in 1703 — Geumjeongsanseong — is the largest fortress in Korea by enclosed area, nearly 9 square kilometers of mountain terrain enclosed by 17 kilometers of stone walls. It was built during the Joseon dynasty in response to the Japanese invasions of the 1590s, which had demonstrated that the peninsula's coastal cities were vulnerable to attack from the sea. The walls were intended to provide a defensible highland position if the coast fell. They were never tested in battle, and the main function they served for most of their history was symbolic — a statement of sovereign territory on a contested frontier.

Most visitors see only the first kilometer of walls near the cable car terminus. The other sixteen kilometers belong to almost no one.

The cable car from Geumgang Park carries visitors to the fortress ridge in a few minutes. From the South Gate, the city below is visible in a way it never is from within it — the residential grid of Dongnae and Busanjin, the industrial flatlands of the port, the sea glinting at the far southern edge. In clear weather you can see across to Japan — the island of Tsushima, 55 kilometers offshore, has been visible from this ridge for as long as people have been on it. The Joseon dynasty built a lookout post here precisely for that reason: to watch for ships from the south. It is, in a literal sense, the place from which Japan first appeared on the Korean horizon.

THE FILM CITY

The Busan International Film Festival — BIFF — began in 1996 as the first large-scale international film festival in Asia, and it became the platform through which Asian cinema gained sustained international critical attention during the late 1990s and 2000s. Directors including Park Chan-wook, Bong Joon-ho, and Hirokazu Kore-eda received significant early BIFF exposure. The festival is held each October, centered on the Busan Cinema Center in Centum City — a complex built specifically for the festival and opened in 2011, distinguished by the 163-meter canopy above the outdoor theater, which carries the world's largest LED display. Outside festival season, the outdoor theater is an empty concrete plaza visible through glass curtain walls. The canopy's lights run programmed displays after dark.

The neighborhood in Nampo-dong associated with the festival's older outdoor screenings is BIFF Square — a pedestrianized section of street named for the festival and marked with handprints of Korean and international directors embedded in the pavement. The square's commercial identity now runs strongly toward street food — particularly ssiat hotteok, a seed-filled sweet pancake deep-fried to order at stalls clustered along the block. The connection to cinema is commemorative rather than operational: the large outdoor screenings that originally defined the square moved to Centum City when the Cinema Center opened.

THE BEACHES

Haeundae Beach's identity as the center of South Korean beach culture is a post-war phenomenon. The district's development as a resort area accelerated in the 1970s and 1980s, as rising incomes made leisure travel possible for middle-class Koreans for the first time. The beach itself is a crescent of fine sand — 1.8 kilometers of it, backed by what became one of the most concentrated clusters of luxury high-rise hotels in Korea. At peak summer, typically in late July and early August, Haeundae Beach can draw a million visitors in a single day. The sea in summer is warm enough to swim comfortably; in winter, the water temperature drops to around 10°C and the beach is largely empty, the hotels half-lit and the sand unoccupied. The off-season version is a different place entirely — the scale of the built environment, which feels appropriate to the crowds in summer, becomes disproportionate when the crowds are gone.

Gwangalli, a kilometer south along the coast, draws a different crowd at a different hour. The beach is smaller and the waterfront has developed into a dense strip of cafés and restaurants rather than resort hotels. The Gwangan Bridge — the twin-span suspension bridge that forms the background of every Gwangalli photograph — was opened in 2003 and runs 7.4 kilometers to connect Suyeong-gu with Haeundae-gu. At night the bridge carries colored lights, and the combination of the lit bridge, the café strip, and the dark water gives Gwangalli its particular atmosphere, which is urban and social in a way that Haeundae, with its resort-hotel scale, is not.

FOOD AND LANGUAGE

Busan's most characteristic foods are products of the Korean War. Dwaeji gukbap — pork-bone broth with sliced pork over rice — was developed from necessity when beef was unavailable and pork offal was cheap. Milmyeon, the cold wheat noodle dish that functions as Busan's answer to the northern naengmyeon, was created when buckwheat couldn't be sourced in the south. Both dishes are still eaten at breakfast, by locals, in restaurants that open before 8 AM and close in the early evening. Both are priced well under ₩15,000 and have no real equivalents anywhere else in South Korea.

The Busan dialect — Gyeongsang-do saturi — is one of the most distinct regional dialects in Korean, characterized by a pitch-accent system that has largely been lost from standard Seoul Korean. The pitch patterns, the vocabulary, and the cadence of Busan dialect are recognizable to any Korean speaker immediately and are associated (in Korean popular culture) with a certain directness and toughness — stereotypes that Busan people tend to wear with some affection. The dialect is most audible in the Nampo and Jagalchi market neighborhoods, where a significant portion of the population is older and less likely to code-switch to standard Korean. Most service interactions in tourist-facing businesses will be in standard Korean or, frequently, in reasonable English.
