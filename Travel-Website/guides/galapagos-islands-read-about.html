Read About Galápagos Islands

An archipelago six hundred miles off the coast of Ecuador that changed how humans understand life on Earth — and still does.

Volcanoes in the middle of the Pacific

The Galápagos Islands are not supposed to be there. The archipelago sits astride the equator, roughly six hundred miles west of continental Ecuador, in a stretch of ocean where the Humboldt Current carries cold, nutrient-rich water up from the Antarctic and the Panama Current delivers warm equatorial water from the north. The collision of these two systems produces an upwelling that feeds one of the densest concentrations of marine life on the planet. The islands themselves arose from a volcanic hot spot in the ocean floor — the same mechanism that built Hawaii — and the western islands are still actively forming. Fernandina, the youngest of the main islands, last erupted in 2024.

There are thirteen major islands, six smaller ones, and more than a hundred islets and rocks, spread across an area roughly the size of Belgium. They were never connected to mainland South America. Every species that lives here today arrived by crossing open ocean — carried on wind or currents or the backs of other animals — and then adapted, over millions of years, to conditions that have no parallel elsewhere on Earth.

What Darwin actually found

Charles Darwin arrived on the HMS Beagle in September 1835, a twenty-six-year-old naturalist on a survey voyage. He spent five weeks in the Galápagos, moving between four of the main islands, and his time here was more confused than the mythology suggests. He didn't arrive with a theory waiting to be confirmed. He collected specimens — birds, tortoises, iguanas, plants — and shipped them back to England largely unlabeled, because it didn't occur to him that the mockingbirds from different islands were different species. A London ornithologist, John Gould, told him otherwise in 1837.

What Darwin found in the Galápagos was not a revelation but a question: if the islands were geologically young and had been colonized from the mainland, why were the species here so different from their South American relatives, and why did they differ from island to island? The answer he eventually developed — that species change over time through natural selection, that individuals better suited to their environment survive and reproduce at higher rates, that given enough time and isolation this process produces entirely new species — required another twenty-two years to work out and publish. On the Origin of Species appeared in 1859.

Darwin spent five weeks here and left uncertain what he had seen. It took him two decades to understand it.

The finches named after him — there are seventeen species, each with a beak adapted to a different food source — are the most famous illustration of his theory, though Darwin paid them less attention during the voyage than he paid the mockingbirds. The variation is real and visible: the large ground finch cracks hard seeds with a thick, strong beak; the woodpecker finch pries insects from bark with a long, pointed one; the cactus finch feeds on cactus flowers and fruit. They all descended from a single South American finch species that colonized the islands roughly two to three million years ago. What happened to them since is evolution made legible.

The animals that don't run

The most immediately striking thing about wildlife in the Galápagos is that it ignores you. Marine iguanas bask on black lava rocks while visitors step around them. Blue-footed boobies perform their mating dance at eye level on the path ahead. Sea lions sleep on benches and boats and, occasionally, in the middle of docks. Nazca boobies build their nests on the trail and hold their ground when you walk past. The animals here have no evolved fear of humans — the islands had no land predators before human arrival, and the birds and reptiles that flourished in their absence never developed the flight instinct that evolution produces in places where something will eat you.

This quality — a kind of focused indifference to human presence — changes what it means to observe wildlife. You are not watching animals from a distance; you are walking among them. A snorkeling session off the coast of Santa Cruz is not a matter of glimpsing fish from above — it is swimming through a school of hammerhead sharks that part around you with complete disinterest, past marine iguanas grazing on algae on the seafloor, past penguins (the only penguin species in the Northern Hemisphere) rocketing through the water at speeds that make human swimming look like standing still.

The tortoise question

Galápagos giant tortoises can live for more than 170 years and weigh up to 550 pounds. There were originally fifteen subspecies, one for each of the major islands and island regions where tortoises were found. Five of those subspecies are now extinct. The survivors number somewhere between twenty thousand and twenty-five thousand individuals, compared to a population that may have been two to three hundred thousand before European contact.

The decline was thorough and deliberate. Whalers and sailors from the seventeenth century onward discovered that giant tortoises could survive for months in the holds of ships without food or water, providing fresh meat on long voyages. Estimates suggest that more than a hundred thousand tortoises were removed from the islands between the 1600s and the 1900s. The introduction of rats, pigs, dogs, and goats — which ate tortoise eggs and competed for food — compounded the damage. By the 1960s, the Pinta Island subspecies had been reduced to a single individual, a male known as Lonesome George, who spent the last forty years of his life as the most famous tortoise in the world and died in 2012 without producing viable offspring.

The recovery programs that have been running since the 1960s have had real success. The Charles Darwin Research Station on Santa Cruz has bred and repatriated thousands of tortoises to islands from which they had been nearly eliminated. The Española Island subspecies was reduced to fourteen individuals; the population is now over two thousand. In 2019, a previously unknown subspecies was discovered in a small population on Fernandina — thought extinct for a century and a half — and breeding efforts began immediately.

Making an island safe from itself

Ecuador declared the Galápagos a national park in 1959, a century after Darwin's theory was published. UNESCO designated the archipelago a World Heritage Site in 1978. These designations came just in time. The human population of the islands had grown rapidly after the Second World War, and the introduced species that arrived with settlers — rats, cats, dogs, pigs, goats, donkeys, black rats, house mice — were doing to island ecosystems what the whalers had done to the tortoises.

The eradication campaigns that followed were among the most ambitious ecological interventions ever attempted. Project Isabela, completed in 2006, removed more than 250,000 feral goats from the island of Isabela using a combination of ground shooting, aerial hunting from helicopters, and a strategy called the Judas goat — a tracked, sterilized animal who led hunters to hidden populations. The project cost millions of dollars and took years. The vegetation on Isabela has recovered visibly since.

The islands' peculiarity is that they show what happens when evolution runs without predators. The recovery programs show what happens when humans try to run it backward.

The human population of the inhabited islands — Santa Cruz, San Cristóbal, Isabela, and Floreana — numbers around thirty thousand people, mostly in the towns of Puerto Ayora, Puerto Baquerizo Moreno, and Puerto Villamil. Tourism is the primary economy. The national park limits the number of visitors to designated sites and requires that tourists move through the islands with licensed naturalist guides, a requirement that has kept the visitor experience more controlled than in almost any comparable destination. The Galápagos receive roughly three hundred thousand visitors per year — a fraction of what a European island of equivalent fame would attract.

What the islands look like

The Galápagos are not conventionally beautiful. The older, eastern islands are drier than they appear on the map, their lowland zones covered in gray-brown scrub and cactus forest, their lava fields still bare and sharp enough to cut through boots. The marine iguanas that crowd the rocky shores are prehistoric and strange-looking — black, with salt-whitened heads where they sneeze out excess sea salt, arrayed on the rocks in writhing masses that look like something out of a Bosch painting.

Beauty accumulates here differently. It comes from specificity — from the moment a blue-footed booby lifts its vivid feet in the courtship display and you understand, watching it, that the brightness is real and selected for, that this is what evolution looks like in operation. It comes from the clarity of the water, which turns from turquoise to deep blue to black over the drop-offs, and from the variety packed into small areas: a half-mile walk on Española can take you past a waved albatross colony, a blowholes formation where the swell forces geysers through lava tubes, and a beach full of sea lions and marine iguanas.

The highland interiors of the larger islands are a different world — green and wet, frequently in cloud, home to Scalesia forests unique to the archipelago. Scalesia trees are in the daisy family; they evolved from a single daisy-like plant and grew, in the absence of competition, into a proper forest. Walking through a Scalesia forest in the cloud zone above Santa Cruz is one of the stranger botanical experiences available to a visitor anywhere.

The permission to exist here

Travel to the Galápagos carries a particular weight. The islands' survival depends on the revenue tourism generates and on the restrictions that tourism brings. Every visitor pays a park entrance fee that funds conservation. Every visitor is limited to designated trails, accompanied by guides, prohibited from taking anything from the islands or leaving anything behind. The inconveniences are real and intentional: the point is not your comfort but the islands' integrity.

What this produces, in practice, is a place where the transaction is explicit. You are paying to enter a functioning ecosystem, and the terms of entry require you to behave as a guest in it rather than a consumer of it. The experience of the Galápagos is unlike almost any other destination in the world not because of what you see — though what you see is extraordinary — but because of the deliberateness of the whole arrangement. Someone decided that these islands mattered enough to protect, and that protection is the reason they are still worth visiting. The awareness of that fact is part of every hour you spend here.
