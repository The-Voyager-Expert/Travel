Read About Cape Town

A city built at the meeting point of two oceans and two centuries of colonization, and rebuilding itself, block by block, around the memory of what apartheid tried to erase.

A refreshment station becomes a city

Cape Town exists because of a vegetable garden. In 1652, the Dutch East India Company sent Jan van Riebeeck to the foot of Table Mountain not to found a colony but to plant one: a supply station where ships rounding the Cape on the long haul to the Indies could take on fresh water, meat, and produce. The garden he planted survives today as the Company's Garden, a shaded strip of oaks and rose beds in the middle of the City Bowl, and it is worth remembering, standing in it, that the entire city grew outward from this single practical requirement — feed the ships, and let the rest follow.

What followed was neither simple nor peaceful. The Khoekhoe people who had grazed cattle on this land for centuries were pushed out within a generation. The Company brought enslaved people from present-day Indonesia, Malaysia, India, and Madagascar to build the settlement and staff its households — the population that would become known as the Cape Malay community, whose cooking, language, and Islamic faith still shape the Bo-Kaap today. By the time the British took the Cape permanently in 1806, Cape Town was already a deeply layered place: Dutch colonial administration, an enslaved and indentured underclass drawn from three continents, and a small mountain that made the whole arrangement visible from almost everywhere at once.

The mountain that never lets you forget where you are

Table Mountain is roughly 260 million years old — older than the Alps, older than the Himalayas, older than almost anything that gets called a mountain. Geologically it is a slab of sandstone sitting on a granite base, worn flat over an immense span of time rather than shaped by any single dramatic event. What makes it strange to stand on is not its age but its biology: the mountain and the surrounding Cape Peninsula hold more plant species per square kilometre than almost anywhere on Earth, packed into the fynbos shrubland that also carpets Kirstenbosch below. Botanists count over 2,200 plant species on the mountain alone — more than the entire United Kingdom in an area a fraction of the size — and a third of them grow nowhere else.

The cloud that spills over the mountain's flat top on a south-easterly wind day has a name — the "tablecloth" — and Capetonians read it the way sailors read weather. The wind itself is called the Cape Doctor, because it is credited, not entirely as a joke, with blowing pollution and disease out of the old town in the days before modern sanitation. It still blows hard enough most summer afternoons to close the cableway and rearrange umbrellas on Camps Bay's restaurant terraces without warning.

The lime quarry on Robben Island has no explanatory plaque. It doesn't need one. The glare alone tells most of the story.

The island and the man

Robben Island had been a place of banishment for three centuries before it became a prison for the leaders of the anti-apartheid movement — Dutch colonists exiled political prisoners from Indonesia and local chiefs here as early as the seventeenth century, and it later served as a leper colony and a naval base. Its most consequential chapter began in 1964, when Nelson Mandela and seven co-defendants were sentenced to life imprisonment at the Rivonia Trial and transferred to the island's maximum-security section.

Mandela spent eighteen of his twenty-seven years in prison on Robben Island, most of it in a cell roughly two metres square, and a significant part of every working day breaking limestone in an open quarry under a sun that, reflected off the pale rock, damaged the eyesight of many prisoners permanently — Mandela's tear ducts were affected for the rest of his life. The island's tours today are led by former political prisoners, a deliberate choice by the museum: the history is narrated by people who lived it, not by professional guides reciting it. It remains one of the only heritage sites in the world where that is standard practice rather than a special occasion.

District Six and the geography of forced removal

Before 1966, District Six was one of Cape Town's most integrated inner-city neighbourhoods — a dense, mixed community of freed slaves, merchants, immigrants, and labourers of every background, famous for its jazz clubs, its street life, and a particular Cape Town humour that residents still describe with real longing. In February 1966 the apartheid government declared it a "whites-only" area under the Group Areas Act. Over the following thirteen years, bulldozers levelled more than 60,000 people's homes, and the government dispersed the community to the sandy, distant townships of the Cape Flats — Mitchells Plain, Bonteheuvel, Lavender Hill — dismantling social networks that had taken generations to build.

Remarkably, once the land was cleared, almost nothing was built on it. International and local pressure, combined with the practical difficulty of laying new foundations on unstable, symbolically radioactive ground, left District Six an open scar in the middle of the city for decades — a strange, grassy void a ten-minute walk from Parliament. The District Six Museum, opened in 1994 in a former Methodist mission church that survived the demolition, exists specifically to hold the street signs, the oral histories, and the maps that former residents drew from memory of streets that no longer exist. Restitution and rebuilding on the original site are still, slowly, ongoing.

Bo-Kaap: colour as an act of refusal

The brightly painted houses of the Bo-Kaap are often photographed as pure decoration, but the colour has a specific, disputed history worth knowing. One widely told account holds that when residents — many of them freed slaves who had been granted the right to own property for the first time — were finally able to buy the houses they had long rented, they painted them in whatever bold colours expressed that new ownership, in a neighbourhood previously required to keep its rented houses uniformly whitewashed. Whether or not every detail of that story holds up to strict historical scrutiny, the neighbourhood's Cape Malay Muslim community, its more than a dozen historic mosques, and its distinctive Afrikaans dialect are not decoration at all — they represent one of the oldest continuously Muslim communities in South Africa, dating to the earliest years of Dutch enslavement at the Cape.

Wine, water, and the Constantia valley

Groot Constantia's vines were planted in 1685, making the estate one of the oldest continuously operating wine producers in the world outside Europe, and its sweet Constantia wine became one of the eighteenth century's genuine international luxury goods — served at the courts of Napoleon (who reportedly requested it in exile on Saint Helena), Frederick the Great, and Louis Philippe, and mentioned by name in novels by Jane Austen and Charles Dickens. Phylloxera and changing tastes ended that first golden age by the late 1800s, but the valley's vineyards, and the restored Cape Dutch manor house at their centre, still trace the same slopes the Dutch East India Company first planted below the mountain's southern face.

A democracy still finding its shape

Cape Town's post-apartheid story is neither a simple triumph nor a simple failure, and the city is honest about that in ways that can surprise first-time visitors. The wealth visible in the City Bowl and the Atlantic Seaboard sits within a short drive of some of the most under-resourced townships in the country, a geography that apartheid planning deliberately built and that thirty years of democracy has only begun to reshape. The city's water crisis of 2017–2018 — when Cape Town came within weeks of becoming the first major world city to run dry, a countdown residents called "Day Zero" — forced an entire population to rethink its relationship with a resource most cities take for granted, and the conservation habits from that crisis largely persist today.

None of this diminishes what makes Cape Town worth visiting; if anything it explains why the city feels different from other places built around a beautiful view. The mountain, the ocean, and the wine valleys are real, but so is the history stacked underneath them, and the best version of a visit here holds both at once.
