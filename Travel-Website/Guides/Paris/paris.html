From Hotel: 🚕 16 min → Sainte-Chapelle
1. Sainte-Chapelle
A 13th-century royal chapel whose upper level is 15 stained-glass walls — the most intact Gothic glass in Europe, built by Louis IX to house the Crown of Thorns. Go on a bright morning when the light fires the windows.

2. Conciergerie
The medieval royal palace turned Revolutionary prison — the Hall of the Men-at-Arms is the largest surviving Gothic secular hall in Europe, and Marie-Antoinette's reconstructed cell marks where she waited for the guillotine.

3. Notre-Dame de Paris
The cathedral France measures itself from — the bronze marker outside the west front is kilometre zero for every road in the country. Begun 1163, gutted by fire and reopened after a five-year restoration that returned the vaults and the interior stone to a pale gold nobody alive had seen. Entry is free.

4. Panthéon
Begun as a church for Sainte Geneviève and taken over by the Revolution as the resting place of the people France decides to honour — Voltaire, Rousseau, Hugo, Zola, Marie Curie, Josephine Baker. Foucault hung his pendulum under the dome in 1851 to prove the earth turns; the replacement still swings. The colonnade above gives one of the best rooftop views in the city.

Day 2
From Hotel: 🚶 25 min · 🚕 9 min → Les Invalides & Napoleon's Tomb
1. Les Invalides & Napoleon's Tomb
Louis XIV built this as a hospital for wounded soldiers in 1670 and crowned it with a gilded dome that is still the tallest in Paris. Under it, Napoleon lies in six nested coffins inside a single block of red quartzite, sunk into an open crypt so that anyone looking at him has to bow. The Musée de l'Armée around it runs from medieval armour to 1945.

2. Musée Rodin
Rodin rented rooms in the Hôtel Biron in 1908 and left the state everything he owned on condition it became his museum. The bronzes stand outdoors in a three-acre garden — The Thinker, The Gates of Hell, The Burghers of Calais — with Camille Claudel's work and Rodin's own Van Gogh and Monet inside.

3. Musée d'Orsay
A 1900 railway station saved from demolition and turned into the greatest Impressionist collection anywhere — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Manet's Déjeuner sur l'herbe, Van Gogh's self-portrait, Whistler's mother. Go straight to the fifth floor first; that is where the Impressionists hang, and where the giant station clock looks out over the Seine.

4. Les Deux Magots
The literary café of Saint-Germain — Sartre and de Beauvoir's daily office, Hemingway's watering hole, Picasso's meeting spot. Order a café crème and the house hot chocolate, poured thick from a silver jug, from the terrace facing the oldest church in Paris.

5. Jardin du Luxembourg
Marie de' Medici built the palace in 1612 because she was homesick for Florence, and the garden still splits the difference between French geometry and Italian terrace. The French Senate sits in the palace; everyone else gets 60 acres of gravel walks, the Medici Fountain in its plane-tree tunnel, and the octagonal basin where children have sailed rented wooden boats since the 1920s.

Day 3
From Hotel: 🚶 38 min · 🚕 13 min → Eiffel Tower
1. Eiffel Tower
Gustave Eiffel's 1889 iron tower — the World's Fair showpiece Paris refused to take down. Still the highest observation deck in the city and the clearest way to read its geography: Seine, La Défense, Montmartre, Montparnasse. Book the summit lift; the second floor is the better view but the top is the reason people queue.

2. Rue Cler · food street stroll
The 7th-arrondissement market street Rick Steves built his Paris chapter around — pedestrianised, two blocks, lined with family-run fromageries, boulangeries, and rôtisseries that have supplied the quartier for generations. Lunch here rather than at the tower.

3. Pont Alexandre III
Built for the 1900 World's Fair and named for a Russian tsar, this is the most extravagant bridge in Paris — gilded Fames on four granite pylons, art-nouveau lamps, cherubs and nymphs the whole way across. A single low steel arch was engineered deliberately so it would not block the view of the Invalides dome behind it. Walk it at dusk on the way back to the hotel.

4. Seine River Cruise · Bateaux-Mouches
The stretch of river between the Pont d'Iéna and the Île Saint-Louis is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and an hour on the water is the only way to see all of it in sequence — Orsay, the Louvre, the Conciergerie, Notre-Dame, and back under the Pont Alexandre III. Book a late-afternoon departure: the monuments light up and the Eiffel Tower sparkles on the hour.

From Hotel: 🚶 34 min · 🚕 14 min → Musée du Louvre
1. Musée du Louvre
The most-visited museum on earth, in a palace that was a fortress before it was a royal residence. 35,000 works on display: the Mona Lisa, the Venus de Milo, the Winged Victory of Samothrace at the top of the Daru staircase, and the Grande Galerie of Italian painting. Pick two wings and accept you will not see the rest.

2. Palais-Royal
Richelieu's 1633 palace, now enclosing the city's finest enclosed garden. Arcaded galleries on three sides house antique dealers, jewellers, and cafés. In the Cour d'Honneur: Daniel Buren's 1986 Les Deux Plateaux — 260 striped columns of varying heights set in granite and water channels.

3. Jardin des Tuileries
André Le Nôtre laid this out in 1664 before he was sent to Versailles, and the logic is the same: gravel allées, clipped hedges, and two round basins on a dead-straight axis running from the Louvre to the Concorde obelisk. The green metal chairs are free and movable — Parisians drag them to the water and stay for an hour.

4. Musée de l'Orangerie
Eight wall-sized Monet Water Lilies canvases in two oval rooms the painter designed — lit from above, no right angles. One of the few museum experiences in Paris that earns the word immersive. Downstairs, the Walter-Guillaume collection holds Cézanne, Renoir, Modigliani, and Soutine.

1. Arc de Triomphe · rooftop
Napoleon ordered it in 1806; it took 30 years to build. The 284-step climb ends on the best axial view in Paris — 12 avenues radiating out, Champs-Élysées to the Louvre one way, La Défense the other. The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier below has had a flame relit every evening at 6:30pm since 1923.

The village Paris absorbed — winding lanes, the Place du Tertre painters' square, and the white travertine Basilique du Sacré-Cœur crowning the hill. Climb for the broadest free view over the whole city; the funicular takes the sting out of the steps and accepts a standard metro ticket.

Place des Vosges, Henri IV's 1612 pink-brick arcaded square, anchors a self-guided wander through Rue des Rosiers and the Village Saint-Paul. Victor Hugo's apartment at no. 6 is free to enter.

1. Château de Versailles
Louis XIV moved the French court out of Paris in 1682 and built the machine that ran it — 2,300 rooms designed so that watching the king get dressed was a political privilege. The State Apartments lead to the Hall of Mirrors: 73 metres, 357 mirrors, 17 windows onto the garden, and the room where the treaty ending the First World War was signed in 1919. UNESCO World Heritage Site.

2. Gardens of Versailles
André Le Nôtre spent 40 years bending 800 hectares into straight lines. The Latona and Apollo fountains step down the central axis to the Grand Canal, where Louis XIV kept a fleet of gondolas; the bosquets on either side are outdoor rooms of hedge and statuary. On Musical Fountains days the whole waterworks runs to Baroque music, as it was designed to.

3. Domaine de Marie-Antoinette · Trianon
The far end of the park, where the court rules did not apply. Louis XIV's pink-marble Grand Trianon was where he escaped his own palace; Marie-Antoinette was given the neighbouring Petit Trianon and built the Hameau de la Reine behind it — a working farm hamlet of thatched cottages, a dairy, and a mill, where she staged the simple life. The walk out across the park takes 25 minutes.

1. Fondation Monet — House & Gardens
Monet bought this house in 1883 and spent 43 years planting the garden that became his greatest painting — the lily pond, the Japanese bridge, and the Clos Normand of irises and roses in his exact colour logic. Having seen the Water Lilies at the Orangerie and the Orsay canvases earlier in the week, this is where they were made. Closed in winter.

Chartres · 1 hr 10 min
The benchmark High Gothic cathedral, completed 1220 and largely untouched since — 176 medieval stained-glass panels in the famous "Chartres blue," a labyrinth set into the nave floor, and two mismatched spires. UNESCO World Heritage Site. Direct TER trains.

Rouen · 1 hr 25 min
Normandy's medieval capital — Rouen Cathedral is the Gothic façade Monet painted 30 times. The half-timbered lanes around the Gros-Horloge are the best-preserved in northern France, and Place du Vieux-Marché marks where Joan of Arc was burned in 1431.
🎫 book at: SNCF Connect or Omio
Strasbourg · 1 hr 48 min
The Franco-German capital of Alsace — Petite France's canals and timbered winemakers' houses are a UNESCO quarter. The pink-sandstone cathedral spire was Europe's tallest until 1874. Compact, walkable, lunch in a winstub.

Lyon · 2 hrs
France's second city and gastronomic capital — Vieux Lyon's Renaissance traboules, the hilltop basilica of Fourvière with Roman-amphitheatre remains, and the silk-weavers' Croix-Rousse. A bouchon lunch is as non-negotiable as any monument.

❗️ Heads Up
Louvre & Musée d'Orsay — Timed slots only
Both sell timed entry and turn walk-ups away at peak times; the Louvre refuses walk-ups outright in July and August.
Notre-Dame — Paid tickets are a scam
Cathedral entry is free. The only booking is a free timed slot on the official site or app; every site selling entry tickets is reselling nothing.
Versailles — Monday closure & noon Trianon
Palace and Trianon estate close every Monday while the gardens stay open. The Trianon does not open until 12:00pm — palace first, Trianon last.
Palais Garnier — Auditorium & security
The auditorium closes often for rehearsals with no warning or refund; timed-entry tickets still face 20–30 min security queues.
Fondation Monet Giverny — Seasonal closure
Monet's house and gardens close from early November to late March — the Giverny day trip does not run in winter.

👑 A Relic Pricier Than Its Chapel, and a Lake That's Really There

Sainte-Chapelle was built to be a jewel box, but the treasure inside it was worth vastly more than the box itself. King Louis IX bought what he believed was Christ's Crown of Thorns from Baldwin II, the cash-strapped Latin emperor of Constantinople, who was pawning off Byzantine relics to pay his own creditors. The relic alone cost several times more than the entire chapel built to display it — the delicate stained-glass masterpiece you're standing in was, financially speaking, just the frame.

The underground lake beneath Palais Garnier isn't a novelist's invention — it's a real cistern, built because the site's groundwater kept flooding the foundation during construction and engineers needed somewhere to redirect it. Crime reporter Gaston Leroux saw it and turned it into the phantom's lair for his novel decades later, but the water tank never stopped serving its actual purpose: Paris firefighters still use it today to train for swimming in total darkness.
