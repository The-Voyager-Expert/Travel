Day 3
From Hotel: 🚕 12 min → Running Eagle Falls
1.Running Eagle Falls
A two-level waterfall where flow shifts with snowmelt: early summer brings a full cascade over the cliff face; later, water emerges from a mid-cliff opening as the upper source diverts underground through porous rock, leaving the top dry. The 0.6-mile loop reaches the falls in 15 minutes. Named for a celebrated Blackfeet warrior, the site has interpretive panels on its cultural history.

2.Two Medicine Lake
A glacier-carved alpine lake set beneath Rising Wolf Mountain and Sinopah Mountain in the quieter southeastern corner of the park. Glacier Park Boat Company runs 1.5-hour narrated tours across the main lake to the Upper Two Medicine landing, giving access to the upper basin without the full hiking distance. The Two Medicine valley sees a fraction of the crowds at Logan Pass or Many Glacier.

3.Scenic Point
A 7.8-mile round-trip ascent gaining 2,238 feet to a bare ridgeline at the edge of the Continental Divide. At the summit the view spans 180 degrees — Glacier's stacked peaks to the west and the Great Plains completely flat to the horizon east. Trail climbs through subalpine fir forest then switchbacks up open terrain to the exposed ridgeline above the Two Medicine valley.

Day 5
From Hotel: 🚕 50 min → Highline Trail
1.Highline Trail
A 7.6-mile one-way traverse from Logan Pass along the Garden Wall, following the Continental Divide at 6,600 feet with mountain goats on the slopes. The first 200 yards cross a narrow cliff ledge with a fixed cable, then open into wide alpine meadows. Granite Park Chalet at the far end — a stone 1914 railway hostel accessible only on foot — is the turnaround before the 4.6-mile Loop Trail descent.

2.Weeping Wall
A seasonal waterfall on the west side of Going-to-the-Sun Road where snowmelt from Haystack Butte seeps through the cliff face and flows over the road below. The flow peaks in June and early July; by late August it tapers to thin threads. Visitors can pull off and walk under the curtain of water — it soaks anyone who steps close — in the final climb to Logan Pass.

🌲 The Hotel Lobby Was Built From Trees Too Big to Grow in Montana

The hotel guests check into on this trip is itself a piece of the park's engineering history. The lodge was built in 1913 by the Great Northern Railway to draw passengers to its new rail line, and its three-story lobby is ringed by sixty Douglas-fir columns each roughly 40 feet tall — timber so massive that Montana's own forests couldn't supply it. Every log was cut in the Pacific Northwest during the specific season when sap runs low, so the bark would stay attached during transport, then shipped in by rail on custom-built cars. A crew of about 75 men spent a year and a half raising the frame using nothing but horses, chains, and pulleys — no cranes, no machinery — which is how the building earned its local nickname, the Big Tree Lodge.

The park's namesake ice has been quietly disappearing for over a century. Around 1850, roughly 150 glaciers carved these valleys; today only about 26 are still named and mapped. Signs at the visitor centers once carried a hard target date for when every glacier would vanish entirely, a projection based on early-2000s climate models — but when the melt rate slowed more than expected, the park quietly pulled those signs and replaced them with a vaguer warning that the timeline "depends on how and when we act." The ice is still retreating, just not on the exact scientific timetable the park itself once posted on its own walls.
