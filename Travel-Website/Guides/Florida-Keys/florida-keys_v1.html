1. Mallory Square Sunset
The nightly sunset gathering at Mallory Square Plaza — street musicians, acrobats, and crowds converging to watch the sun drop into the Gulf of Mexico. Vendors line the square and visitors applaud at the moment the sun touches the horizon. A longstanding Key West tradition at the western tip of Duval Street.

2. Duval Street Historic District
A mile-long street lined with historic buildings from Key West's era of shipwrecking and cigar manufacturing. Duval runs from the Gulf to the Atlantic and is the spine of the old city — Conch-style colonial homes, galleries, and the most concentrated strip of 19th-century commercial architecture in South Florida.

3. Fort Jefferson
A 19th-century brick fortress 70 miles west of Key West, accessible by ferry. The hexagonal fortification sits in Dry Tortugas National Park and was used as a military prison holding Dr. Samuel Mudd. Visitors snorkel among coral reefs and walk the ramparts for unobstructed Gulf views.

The largest undeveloped beach in the Keys, with white sand and shallow water. The park spans the island and includes the old Bahia Honda Rail Bridge from the Flagler Railroad. The beach is ideal for swimming and snorkeling just offshore, and is notably more spacious than Key West beaches.

Housed in a replica of a 19th-century wrecker's warehouse, the museum documents Key West's history as a wrecking capital through salvaged artifacts: cannons, anchors, gold coins, and a lighthouse lens. A climbing tower offers views over Key West harbor and the reef where hundreds of ships wrecked.

From Hotel: 🚕 15 min → Ernest Hemingway Home and Museum
1. Ernest Hemingway Home and Museum
A Spanish Colonial Revival home where Hemingway lived from 1931 to 1940 and wrote For Whom the Bell Tolls and A Farewell to Arms. The limestone house with verandah and courtyard pool is home to 40 to 50 polydactyl six-toed cats, each named after a famous figure — descendants of Hemingway's original cats. National Historic Landmark; walk-in admission at the door.

2. Key West Lighthouse
A 110-foot brick lighthouse built in 1848 after a hurricane destroyed the original 1825 structure. Eighty-eight steps climb through the cast-iron tower to a lantern room with panoramic views over Old Town Key West rooftops and both the Gulf and Atlantic waters visible from a single vantage point. The keeper's quarters are a preserved Victorian cottage with period furnishings.

3. Harry S. Truman Little White House
President Truman spent 175 vacation days at this Colonial Revival building on the Key West Naval Station grounds — more than any president at a single retreat. Truman conducted Berlin Airlift negotiations here in 1948. The interior retains original period furnishings, the oval study, and the poker table where Truman played cards each morning with his staff.

4. Mel Fisher Maritime Museum
Over 200,000 artifacts from the 1622 Spanish galleon Nuestra Señora de Atocha are on display, including gold bars, emeralds, and coins from the 40-ton cargo recovered after Mel Fisher's 16-year search. An operating conservation lab allows visitors to observe artifact cleaning and preservation in progress alongside the exhibit floor.

Day 5
From Hotel: 🚕 50 min → Pigeon Key
1. Pigeon Key
A five-acre island under the old Seven Mile Bridge, accessible only by ferry. The Flagler Railroad established its construction workers' camp here in 1908; seven original wood-frame buildings survive as a National Historic District. The ferry crosses the original 1912 bridge span, with views of both the old and new Seven Mile Bridge running in parallel.

2. Turtle Hospital
The world's only fully licensed veterinary hospital for sea turtles, treating loggerhead, green, Kemp's ridley, and hawksbill turtles injured by boat strikes, monofilament entanglement, and cold stunning. Guided tours walk through outdoor rehabilitation tanks where recovering patients receive care before release. More than 3,000 sea turtles have been treated since 1986.

3. Crane Point Museum and Nature Center
A 65-acre preserve protecting intact tropical hardwood hammock in the Florida Keys. Trails lead to the 1903 Adderley House, a Bahamian immigrant dwelling built with wooden pegs and notched beams, and to pre-Columbian Indian middens from the Calusa occupation of the Keys dating to 500–1000 AD. Walk-in admission at the entrance.

🌊 The Road Built on a Destroyed Railroad

The Overseas Highway — the 113-mile stretch of US-1 that connects the Keys — was built on the foundations of Henry Flagler's Florida East Coast Railway. Flagler completed the railway to Key West in 1912 at the age of 82, a project so audacious it was called the Eighth Wonder of the World. The Labor Day Hurricane of 1935, one of the most intense storms ever to make landfall in the United States, destroyed 40 miles of track and killed hundreds of workers who were trapped on the islands waiting for an evacuation train. The railway was never rebuilt. The bridges were converted to roadway, and the Overseas Highway opened in 1938. Some of the original Flagler bridge stanchions are still visible running parallel to the modern highway as you drive south.

The Florida Keys also hold the only living coral barrier reef ecosystem in the continental United States — the Florida Reef Tract, stretching about 170 miles along the Atlantic side of the archipelago. The Keys themselves are not coral: they are ancient coral reef deposits from a period when sea levels were higher, now exposed as limestone islands. The living reef offshore is a separate, younger structure, and one of the most biodiverse marine environments in the Northern Hemisphere.
