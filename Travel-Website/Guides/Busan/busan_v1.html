🍮 Local Tastes
Dwaeji Gukbap
Busan's signature breakfast dish — simmered pork-bone broth served with tender sliced pork and a bowl of rice; developed from necessity in the Korean War era, now the city's most defining food.
📍 Ssangdung Dwaeji Gukbap · Saemteo
Milmyeon
Cold wheat noodles in chilled beef or chicken broth — Busan's answer to naengmyeon, created when buckwheat was unavailable during the war. Served with a small cup of spicy sauce on the side.
📍 Choryang Milmyeon · Gaya Milmyeon
Ssiat Hotteok
A Busan twist on the classic sweet pancake: filled with mixed seeds, nuts, honey, and cinnamon, then deep-fried until the exterior is crisp. Sold at street stalls on BIFF Square in Nampo-dong.
📍 BIFF Square · Nampo-dong

🌊 A city built on displacement

Busan's identity was forged in the Korean War. The Gamcheon Culture Village, now photographed by millions, was built in the early 1950s by followers of the Taegukdo religious sect who fled south ahead of the war front. The terraced hillside houses — built with whatever materials were available — were later joined by tens of thousands of other refugees, making Gamcheon one of the most densely populated urban areas on the peninsula. Its transformation into an art district came in 2009, but the original structure, the steep stairways between houses, the rooftop-on-rooftop city form — all of that is still the refugee settlement exactly as it was built.

The city's relationship with the sea runs deeper than tourism. Busan has the busiest container port in South Korea and the fifth-busiest in the world. The ship traffic visible from Taejongdae's cliffs or Dongbaekseom's promontory is not scenery — it is the literal engine of the national economy moving through the harbor. The same port was the last point of evacuation during the Korean War. The city that nearly fell is now the city that keeps everything moving.

Geumjeongsanseong's cable car is rarely crowded compared to the fortress's scale — most visitors to the Dongnae area don't bother with the hike. At the top, the stone walls run for 17 kilometers over the mountain ridgeline, but almost no one walks more than the first kilometer toward the North Gate. On a clear morning, the view from the South Gate looks directly down into the city's residential grid and out to the sea beyond — a perspective few visitors to Busan ever see.
