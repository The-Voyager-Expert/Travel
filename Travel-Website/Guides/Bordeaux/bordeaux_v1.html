Day 1
From Hotel: 🚶 8 min · 🚕 3 min → Place de la Bourse
1. Place de la Bourse
Three 18th-century limestone palaces designed by Gabriel for Louis XV, forming Bordeaux's grandest waterfront ensemble and a UNESCO World Heritage site. The Miroir d'Eau in front — 3,450 m² of shallow water — mirrors the baroque facades perfectly.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Open 24/7
⏰ ~45 min
⚠️ Miroir d'Eau operates April–October, 10:00am - 10:00pm only.
📍 Place de la Bourse · Saint-Pierre
🚶 5 min · 🚕 2 min → Porte Cailhau
2. Porte Cailhau
A 35-metre triumphal arch built in 1495 to honour Charles VIII's victory at Fornovo. Climb four medieval floors through Gothic tracery and corbelled battlements to a rooftop terrace overlooking the Garonne. Capacity is 19 visitors; narrow spiral stairs are part of the experience.
📖 Wikipedia
🎟️ Porte Cailhau · 4.5⭐ · porte-cailhau.com
🏛️ Daily 10:00am - 1:00pm · 2:00pm - 6:00pm
⏰ ~45 min
⚠️ November–March: closed Monday and Tuesday; open Wednesday–Sunday.
📍 Place du Palais · Saint-Pierre
🚶 8 min · 🚕 3 min → Grosse Cloche
3. Grosse Cloche
One of France's oldest and best-preserved medieval belfries, built as the city's main gateway in the 15th century. Its 7,800 kg bourdon bell still rings five times a year. Twin round turrets flank the arch — the last remnant of the city's fortified wall on this side of the old town.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Open 24/7
⏰ ~20 min
📍 53 Rue Saint-James · Saint-Éloi
🚶 10 min · 🚕 4 min → Basilique Saint-Michel
4. Basilique Saint-Michel
Bordeaux's largest Gothic church, built 1350–1532, with a 114-metre detached stone spire — the third tallest in France. The spire stands separately because the marshy ground could not support its weight beside the nave. The church anchors the lively Saint-Michel quarter.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Daily 9:00am - 6:30pm
⏰ ~45 min
⚠️ Detached spire open July–September only. Outside that window, spire is viewable from the square only.
📍 Place Canteloup · Saint-Michel

Day 2
From Hotel: 🚶 12 min · 🚕 4 min → Pey-Berland Tower
1. Pey-Berland Tower
The 15th-century detached bell tower of Saint-André cathedral, built separately to spare it from its bells' vibrations. Timed-entry tickets required. The 229-step climb reaches a rooftop terrace at 50 metres — the best panoramic view in Bordeaux, sweeping across the limestone rooftops and the Garonne.
📖 Wikipedia
🎟️ Tour Pey-Berland · 4.5⭐ · pey-berland.fr
🏛️ Daily 10:00am - 1:00pm · 2:00pm - 6:00pm
⏰ ~1 hr
⚠️ Closed January 1, May 1 and December 25. Book timed-entry tickets in advance online.
📍 Place Pey-Berland · Hôtel de Ville
🚶 12 min · 🚕 4 min → Palais Gallien
2. Palais Gallien
The only surviving Roman monument in Bordeaux — a 2nd-century amphitheater that held 15,000 spectators when the city was the Roman Burdigala. Its dramatic limestone arches rise from the urban fabric, the oldest standing structure in the city. Open June–September only; exterior visible year-round.
📖 Wikipedia
🎟️ Palais Gallien · 4.1⭐ · bordeaux-tourisme.com
🏛️ Daily 1:00pm - 6:00pm
⏰ ~30 min
⚠️ Open June–September only. October–May: exterior visible from street at no charge.

3. Cité du Vin
The world's only museum dedicated to the universal culture of wine, housed in an XTU-designed golden tower on the Garonne. Ten permanent galleries trace wine's 8,000-year history from ancient Mesopotamia to the present. The 8th-floor Belvédère ends the visit with a glass of regional wine and a panoramic view.
📖 Wikipedia

⏰ ~2 hrs 30 min
⚠️ November–April: closes 6:00pm.

1. Musée d'Aquitaine
One of France's largest regional history museums, the Musée d'Aquitaine traces 25,000 years in the Bordeaux region. Its star object is the Laussel Venus bas-relief carved around 25,000 BC; a permanent exhibition documents Bordeaux's role as a principal Atlantic slave trade port.
📖 Wikipedia

🚫 Closed Monday

⚠️ Closed on public holidays. Free entry first Sunday of each month — except July and August.

2. Chartrons District
Once the quarter of Dutch, Irish, and English wine merchants who shipped Bordeaux wines across Europe, Chartrons was revived in the early 2000s as the city's antiques and art hub. Rue Notre-Dame is lined with dealers trading Louis XVI furniture and Belle Époque jewellery alongside contemporary galleries.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Open 24/7
⏰ ~1 hr 30 min

3. Jardin Public
Created in 1746 under Intendant de Tourny, Jardin Public is the oldest public park in Bordeaux, classified as a Jardin Remarquable de France. Its 10 hectares contain a Natural History Museum, a historic botanical garden, a children's puppet theatre, and a bar-restaurant inside the restored orangerie.
📖 Wikipedia

⏰ ~45 min

4. Darwin Ecosystem
Darwin is a 9-hectare conversion of the 1874 Caserne Niel military barracks on the Garonne's right bank into an urban campus mixing eco-startups, an organic restaurant, a covered skatepark with leading street-art walls, and an urban farm. The riverside terrace bar is one of the best informal hangouts in Bordeaux.

1. Grand Théâtre de Bordeaux
Designed by Victor Louis and inaugurated in 1780, one of Europe's finest neoclassical opera houses. Its 12-column Corinthian portico directly inspired Charles Garnier's Paris Opéra. Guided tours access the auditorium ceiling fresco, backstage corridors, and rehearsal rooms not open to audiences.
📖 Wikipedia

🚫 Closed Sunday - Friday
⏰ ~1 hr

🚶 6 min · 🚕 2 min → Place des Quinconces
2. Place des Quinconces
Europe's largest public square at 12 hectares, dominated by the Monument aux Girondins — a 54-metre column topped with a figure of Liberty, flanked by 1902 bronze fountains honouring the Girondins deputies executed during the Terror. The esplanade descends to the Garonne quays.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Open 24/7
⏰ ~30 min
📍 Place des Quinconces · Grand Parc
🚶 18 min · 🚕 5 min → Basilique Saint-Seurin
3. Basilique Saint-Seurin
One of Bordeaux's oldest Christian sites, built on a 1st-century Roman necropolis. The 11th-century Romanesque basilica conceals a 5th-century episcopal necropolis — carved sarcophagi and early Christian inscriptions still in situ. A UNESCO World Heritage site on the Camino de Santiago.
📖 Wikipedia
🏛️ Daily 8:00am - 7:00pm
⏰ ~45 min
⚠️ Photography restricted during services. Crypt open seasonally.
📍 Place des Martyrs de la Résistance · Saint-Seurin

4. Musée des Beaux-Arts
One of France's oldest fine-arts museums, founded in 1801 by Napoleon. The collection spans the 16th to 20th century: Rubens, Titian, Veronese, Delacroix, and Matisse. Permanent collection is free; the museum occupies two wings flanking the Hôtel de Ville gardens.
📖 Wikipedia

1. Église Monolithique
The largest rock-hewn church in Europe, excavated from a single limestone cliff face between the 8th and 12th centuries. The entire nave — 38 metres long, 20 metres high — was carved by hand with no freestanding walls. Access by guided tour only from the Tourism Office.
📖 Wikipedia

2. Tour du Roy
The tallest surviving medieval tower in Saint-Émilion, built by Louis VIII in 1224 as a royal keep asserting French authority over this newly-acquired Plantagenet territory. The 35-metre climb gives the best panoramic view over the UNESCO vineyard landscape.
📖 Wikipedia

3. Cloître des Cordeliers
A 14th-century Franciscan cloister whose Gothic arcade is still largely intact above ground. Below it, limestone cellars excavated over centuries now produce Crémant de Bordeaux sparkling wine. The cellar visit includes a tasting in the vaulted gallery where Franciscan monks once stored their provisions.

💀 Jules Verne and Victor Hugo Once Came to See the Mummies Under This Church

In 1791, when Bordeaux cleared the burial grounds around its parish churches, workers digging beneath Basilique Saint-Michel uncovered 74 bodies that had naturally mummified in the ground — no embalming, just something about the clay-rich soil and local conditions that preserved skin and features intact. The find became one of the city's biggest tourist draws for nearly two centuries, displayed in a deliberately eerie crypt setting beneath the tower and pulling in visitors that reportedly included Victor Hugo and Jules Verne, both of whom found the display strange enough to write about. Exposure to open air eventually caused the remains to deteriorate, and in 1979 they were sealed in coffins and reburied anonymously in a nearby cemetery. A video projection in the crypt today is the only trace left of one of Bordeaux's strangest attractions.

Darwin Ecosystem exists because nobody stopped the people who broke in. The site was a military barracks until the army abandoned it in 2005, after which squatters, graffiti artists, and skaters moved into the empty buildings before any official plan existed for the space. Rather than evict them, a local entrepreneur built an actual redevelopment project around what was already happening there, officially launching it in 2009 on a date chosen to mark Charles Darwin's 200th birthday. The indoor skatepark inside was built almost entirely from salvaged construction materials, and the grounds now also host a working urban farm — food grown on the same land where the occupation that saved the site began.
